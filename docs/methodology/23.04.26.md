## **Comparing Metadata Quality Across Heterogeneous Systems**

When both a national journal platform and a global index have gaps or inconsistencies, comparison must rely on explicit quality dimensions, shared identifiers, and careful cross-checking rather than assuming either is a gold standard. Recent work on Crossref, OpenAlex, Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, and repositories offers concrete strategies.

## **Key Quality Dimensions and Operational Definitions**

Commonly used dimensions include **completeness, accuracy, consistency, accessibility, conformance, provenance, and timeliness**  (Kumar et al., 2024; Šlibar & Mu, 2022; Strecker, 2021).  
- **Completeness**: proportion of records with a given field populated (e.g., title, abstract, language, funder)  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Kumar et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Rajesh et al., 2021; Johnston et al., 2024; Šlibar & Mu, 2022; Strecker, 2021).  
- **Accuracy**: agreement with the article PDF/online version, using categories such as full information, partially/fully missing, and non‑matching information  (Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Šlibar & Mu, 2022).  
- **Consistency**: internal coherence and adherence to local rules (e.g., how “publication year” or “document type” is defined and applied)  (Mongeon et al., 2025; Šlibar & Mu, 2022; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  

## **Practical Comparison Workflow**

### Using a Third or Primary Source
- Draw a sample of DOIs (or other persistent IDs) from a neutral backbone like Crossref or from the national platform, then locate matching records in the global index  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Hendricks et al., 2020; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- For each shared record, compare field presence (completeness) and values (accuracy) against the article itself or against a third source where feasible  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Strecker, 2021).  

### Simple Cross-System Metrics

| Aspect compared                 | Example metric                             | Citations |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|-----------|
| Field completeness              | % records with non‑empty field            |  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Rajesh et al., 2021; Johnston et al., 2024; Šlibar & Mu, 2022; Strecker, 2021)|
| Value agreement (per field)     | % records in “full information” category  |  (Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; C'espedes et al., 2024; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026)|
| Discrepancy patterns            | Counts of year/type/language mismatches   |  (Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; C'espedes et al., 2024; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026)|

## **Handling Incomplete / Inconsistent Metadata**

- Treat **neither system as truth**; classify each field as: correct in A, correct in B, both, or neither, based on document inspection or policy logic  (Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- Explicitly document database policies (e.g., selective vs comprehensive coverage, “online first” vs issue year) and use them when interpreting discrepancies  (Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- Use **error typologies** (complete missing, partial missing, non‑matching) to prioritize fixes and to distinguish omissions from false attributions  (Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Šlibar & Mu, 2022; Strecker, 2021).  

## Conclusion

Comparing metadata quality across a national platform and a global index with imperfect data is feasible if done dimension‑by‑dimension, using persistent identifiers, explicit completeness/accuracy measures, and clear knowledge of each system’s policies. No single source should be assumed correct; instead, discrepancies are analyzed, categorized, and, where possible, resolved against the underlying documents or an independent backbone like Crossref.

# **Metadata Quality in Digital Libraries and Scholarly Databases**

Metadata quality directly affects discovery, reuse, interoperability, and trust in digital libraries and scholarly databases. Research converges on a core set of dimensions and several widely used evaluation frameworks and tools.

## Key Quality Dimensions and Metrics

A scoping review finds **completeness, accuracy, consistency, accessibility, conformance, provenance, and timeliness** as the most commonly measured dimensions of metadata quality  (Kumar et al., 2024; Park, 2009; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Chen et al., 2011; , 2021; Park & Tosaka, 2010).  
The Bruce & Hillmann framework, highly influential in this area, defines seven dimensions: **completeness, accuracy, conformance to expectations, logical consistency/coherence, accessibility, timeliness, provenance**  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; , 2021).

- **Completeness**: proportion of mandatory/expected fields filled  (Ochoa & Duval, 2009; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Chen et al., 2011; Osman et al., 2023; , 2021; Bönisch et al., 2024)- **Accuracy/Correctness**: agreement with trusted sources or ground truth  (Park, 2009; Chen et al., 2011; Osman et al., 2023; Zavalin & Zavalina, 2025; Zavalin & Zavalina, 2023; Bönisch et al., 2024)- **Consistency**: internal logical coherence and uniform use of values, formats, vocabularies  (Kumar et al., 2024; Park, 2009; Chen et al., 2011; Osman et al., 2023; Park & Tosaka, 2010; Zavalin & Zavalina, 2023; Bönisch et al., 2024)- **Provenance**: presence and clarity of source and creation information  (Kumar et al., 2024; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Chen et al., 2011; Peng et al., 2022)- **Accessibility**: whether metadata are retrievable and understandable (human/machine)  (Kumar et al., 2024; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Chen et al., 2011; , 2021; Bönisch et al., 2024)- **Timeliness**: currency of records  (Kumar et al., 2024; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Chen et al., 2011; Peng et al., 2022; Bönisch et al., 2024)### Common Dimensions Used in Practice

## Frameworks and Evaluation Approaches

### General Metadata Quality Frameworks
- **Bruce & Hillmann curation framework** underpins many later metric sets  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Chen et al., 2011; , 2021; Zavalin & Zavalina, 2023).  
- Functional perspective tying quality to discovery, use, provenance, currency, authentication, administration  (Park, 2009).

### Automatic / Hybrid Assessment
- Ochoa & Duval define scalable metrics (e.g., **Text Information Content**) and show good correlation with human judgments; combined metrics can filter low‑quality records as effectively as people  (Ochoa & Duval, 2009).  
- Chen et al. adapt Bruce & Hillmann to element‑level metrics (conformance, provenance, accuracy, completeness, accessibility) for cross‑repository Dublin Core  (Chen et al., 2011).  
- MetaEnhance uses AI to detect/correct incomplete, inconsistent, incorrect ETD metadata, evaluated via precision/recall/F1 on curated benchmarks  (Choudhury et al., 2023).

## FAIR and FAIR-Related Assessment

- Multiple initiatives define **FAIR metrics** for data and metadata; indicators include persistent identifiers, rich machine‑actionable metadata, use of community standards, licenses, and provenance  (Peng et al., 2024; Gaignard et al., 2023; Musen et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022; Emonet et al., 2022).  
- **FAIR-Checker** automatically evaluates FAIR metrics using SPARQL/SHACL, reporting missing and recommended metadata and completeness vs. community profiles  (Gaignard et al., 2023).  
- **FAIR-QMM** (FAIRness Quality Maturity Matrix) defines a tiered, cross-tool framework and consolidated FAIR vocabulary for harmonized FAIRness scoring  (Peng et al., 2024).  
- FAIR-DQI guidelines emphasize documenting quality information (completeness, accuracy, provenance, timeliness, stewardship aspects) in FAIR ways  (Peng et al., 2022).  
- FAIR+R adds a **Reliability** block and proposes measures: completeness, consistency, correctness, correspondence, relevance, semantic specificity, timeliness, accessibility, reproducibility  (Bönisch et al., 2024).

## Domain-Specific and LOD/Digital Library Models

- Digital library quality model (5S-based) introduces broader dimensions (accessibility, completeness, consistency, effectiveness, efficiency, reliability, timeliness, etc.) with numerical indicators like response time or number of service failures, applied to catalogs, collections, metadata specifications, and services  (Gonçalves et al., 2007).  
- Linked Open Data (LOD) in digital libraries is evaluated using data quality metrics tailored to RDF triples (e.g., validity of URIs, use of authority files, structural constraints), adapting general LOD quality methodologies  (Candela et al., 2020).  
- Studies of ETD repositories and museum/arts metadata operationalize completeness, accuracy, and consistency into detailed error categories to analyze element‑level problems  (Choudhury et al., 2023; Osman et al., 2023; Zavalin & Zavalina, 2025; Zavalin & Zavalina, 2023).

## Summary

Across digital libraries and scholarly databases, metadata quality is typically measured using dimensions like **completeness, accuracy, consistency, provenance, accessibility, and timeliness**, often grounded in the Bruce & Hillmann framework. These dimensions are operationalized through field‑fill rates, error counts against authorities, consistency checks, and provenance/accessibility indicators, with growing use of automated and hybrid methods. FAIR‑oriented frameworks and tools (FAIR metrics, FAIR-QMM, FAIR-Checker, FAIR-DQI, FAIR+R) extend this by embedding quality assessment within FAIR and reliability principles, emphasizing machine‑actionable, community‑standardized, and well‑documented metadata quality information.

# **OAI-PMH Metadata Harvesting:** Common Limitations and Quality Problems

OAI-PMH is widely used to expose and harvest metadata from institutional repositories and journal platforms, but practice shows many technical and content-related weaknesses.

## **Protocol- and Infrastructure-Level Limitations**

- **Loose specification / heterogeneity:** OAI-PMH does not strictly constrain data structure or value vocabularies, so repositories implement Dublin Core and other formats very differently, making integration hard  (Novytskyi, 2024; Goncharov & Kolosov, 2022; Dunsire, 2008; Jackson et al., 2008).  
- **Limited selectivity & identifiers:** OAI-PMH offers only coarse filtering and no dereferenceable HTTP URIs for records; access is tied to protocol verbs and opaque identifiers  (Haslhofer & Schandl, 2008).  
- **Failure and instability:** Large-scale studies of thousands of OAI-PMH services report very high failure rates (around 44% of harvesting tasks), incomplete result sets, and varied error messages (temporary and permanent)  (Kapidakis, 2019). Many repositories listed in meta-catalogs are non‑operational or outdated  (Gaudinat et al., 2017).  
- **Discovery of endpoints:** OAI-PMH base URLs are often poorly documented and inconsistent, making repositories difficult to find and harvest at scale  (Piedboeuf et al., 2023; Gaudinat et al., 2017).

## **Metadata Quality Issues in Repositories / Journals**

- **Incomplete fields:** Many institutional repositories and journal platforms omit key Dublin Core elements (e.g., subject, description, creator) or provide very sparse records  (Medrano, 2017; Piedboeuf et al., 2023; Dunsire, 2008; Jackson et al., 2008).  
- **Lack of normalization:** Free-text, uncontrolled vocabularies and inconsistent encodings for language, type, format, and subject reduce interoperability and search precision  (Medrano, 2017; Goncharov & Kolosov, 2022; Dunsire, 2008).  
- **Inconsistent or erroneous values:** Studies report incorrect field use, subject errors, legacy anomalies, and changing cataloging practices over time that produce mixed-quality records within one repository  (Piedboeuf et al., 2023; Goncharov & Kolosov, 2022; Dunsire, 2008; Nichols et al., 2009; Jackson et al., 2008).  
- **Missing or weak domain-specific metadata:** Disciplinary repositories (e.g., LIS, chemistry) often lag in rich, domain-appropriate schemas and show “not up to global standards” quality  (Roy et al., 2016; Neumann et al., 2023).  
- **Author-/user-generated metadata issues:** Reliance on depositors without strong guidance leads to omissions, inconsistent formats, and variability between repositories  (Piedboeuf et al., 2023; Dunsire, 2008).

## **Aggregation, Legacy, and Curation Challenges**

- **Legacy schemas and local practices:** Aggregators must cope with idiosyncratic historical decisions; harvest workflows often require per-repository tailoring  (Khoo et al., 2013; Goncharov & Kolosov, 2022).  
- **Cross-repository harmonization:** Merging multiple formats into a common profile is complex and error-prone; both programmatic and manual curation are needed to fix mappings and clean data  (Roy et al., 2018; Dunsire, 2008; Nichols et al., 2009).  
- **Meta-catalog fragmentation:** Major OAI-PMH meta-catalogs share under 1% of repositories in common and each holds many unique, sometimes low‑quality or inactive records, complicating global discovery  (Gaudinat et al., 2017).  

## **Summary**

Across institutional repositories and journal platforms, OAI-PMH harvesting suffers from unstable endpoints, high error rates, weak selectivity, and lack of dereferenceable identifiers. At the metadata level, incompleteness, inconsistent field usage, lack of normalization, and legacy heterogeneity are widespread. Aggregators must invest heavily in mapping, validation, and curation to make harvested metadata reliable and interoperable.

# **Metadata ingestion in large aggregators:** open pipelines, heterogeneous sources, and recurring quality problems

Large scholarly aggregators build massive indexes by harvesting publisher and repository metadata, often via Crossref, and then enriching, normalizing, and deduplicating it. This scale and heterogeneity create systematic error patterns that users need to understand and correct for.

## **How metadata are ingested and built up**

### Crossref as a primary source
- Crossref **receives raw, uncurated metadata from publishers** (titles, authors, affiliations, dates, references, etc.) and does not systematically clean or validate it; errors and omissions at publisher level propagate into downstream systems  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Borrego & Urbano, 2025).  
- It exposes metadata via open APIs (REST, OAI‑PMH) and supports rich fields (citations, funding, corrections, retractions, licenses, etc.)  (Hendricks et al., 2020).  

### OpenAlex’s multi‑source aggregation
- OpenAlex combines Microsoft Academic Graph legacy data with **Crossref and other open sources**, plus web crawling, then assigns document types, subjects, institutions, and concepts algorithmically  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Scheidsteger & Haunschild, 2022; Priem et al., 2022; Borrego & Urbano, 2025; Rizzetto & Peroni, 2023).  
- It must **disambiguate and standardize** works, authors, venues, and institutions across heterogeneous inputs, and handle legal constraints (e.g., abstracts only as inverted indexes, removal of some abstracts)  (Culbert et al., 2024; Alperin et al., 2024; Borrego & Urbano, 2025).  
- Active post‑processing includes syncing millions of records with Crossref, cleaning author strings, and deleting empty author entities  (Culbert et al., 2024).  

### Proprietary aggregators (Scopus, Web of Science)
- Scopus and WoS rely more on **selective venue policies and editorial curation**, applying controlled document‑type taxonomies and internal rules for years, languages, and affiliations  (Culbert et al., 2024; Alperin et al., 2024; Ortega & Delgado-Quirós, 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- They still ingest from publisher feeds and inherit upstream errors (e.g., DOI duplication, incorrect dates, missing funding), though at generally lower rates than OpenAlex  (Ortega & Delgado-Quirós, 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  

### Inter‑system mapping and enrichment
- Aggregators interlink with other infrastructures (OpenCitations, OpenCitations Meta, ORCID, AJOL, etc.), aligning via DOIs, ISSNs and internal IDs; mapping exercises reveal duplicate entities and model differences (e.g., how versions/preprints are grouped or split)  (Rizzetto & Peroni, 2023).  
- APIs are the main delivery channel; many analyses combine multiple APIs (Crossref + OpenAlex + Dimensions, etc.) to mitigate coverage and metadata gaps in any single source  (Velez-Estevez et al., 2023; Jahn, 2025; Ortega & Delgado-Quirós, 2024).  

## **Common metadata errors and inconsistencies**

### Core bibliographic fields
- **Missing or partial bibliographic data**: third‑party databases like OpenAlex often lose volume, issue, and page information when integrating multiple sources (e.g., missing volume in 62% and pages in 51% of sampled OpenAlex records)  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024).  
- **Publication year discrepancies**: inconsistent use of “online first” vs. issue year between OpenAlex and WoS; policies differ and cause systematic year shifts  (Mongeon et al., 2025; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- **Document type misclassification**: OpenAlex frequently over‑labels non‑article material as “articles/reviews” and treats conference papers as journal articles distinguished only by source, leading to large cross‑database discrepancies  (Ortega & Delgado-Quirós, 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Haupka et al., 2024; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  

### Authorship, affiliations, and identifiers
- **Institutional affiliations missing or incomplete**: more than 60% of OpenAlex journal articles lack full institutional information, with strong biases by field, time period, region, and publisher  (Alperin et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- **Author disambiguation and ORCID over‑assignment**: OpenAlex shows very high ORCID coverage but some IDs are incorrectly attached to tens of thousands of records, indicating problems in author clustering and ORCID matching  (Culbert et al., 2024).  
- Proprietary databases also show **missing or mislinked author‑affiliation data**, though generally at lower rates (e.g., earlier WoS snapshots with >20% missing author addresses; incomplete affiliation links later corrected)  (Mongeon et al., 2025).  

### Language, abstracts, and classification
- **Language field errors**: OpenAlex offers broader linguistic coverage than WoS but its language detection mislabels many records, overestimating English and underestimating other languages  (C'espedes et al., 2024; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- **Missing abstracts**: OpenAlex has fewer abstracts than Scopus/WoS and suffers from losses due to legal restrictions and publisher practices; some large publishers do not expose abstracts via Crossref, limiting downstream completeness  (Culbert et al., 2024; Hendricks et al., 2020; Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  
- **Subject and document‑type taxonomies**: algorithmic concepts/topics in OpenAlex lack the detailed curated categorization of Scopus/WoS, producing coarse or inconsistent classifications that affect field‑normalized indicators  (C'espedes et al., 2024; Alperin et al., 2024; Scheidsteger & Haunschild, 2022; Ortega & Delgado-Quirós, 2024; Torres-Salinas & Arroyo-Machado, 2026).  

### References, citations, and retractions
- **Incomplete or inconsistent reference lists**:  
  - OpenAlex’s internal reference coverage is comparable to WoS/Scopus for shared recent corpora, but it does not systematically include **all non‑source references or full reference strings**, limiting custom matching and analysis  (Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
  - OpenAlex often misses citation links, especially for works not also in Scopus/WoS, which constrains inclusive citation analyses  (Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- **Reference count discrepancies**: Scopus‑reported reference counts sometimes do not match actual references in its database; OpenAlex also shows inconsistent handling of specific reference types  (Culbert et al., 2024).  
- **Retraction and status labeling errors**:  
  - Non‑selective databases (OpenAlex, Dimensions, Scilit, The Lens) index more retracted/withdrawn material than selective ones, but inaccurate or inconsistent labeling is common and can inflate apparent coverage  (Ortega & Delgado-Quirós, 2024).  
  - In OpenAlex, a flawed aggregation of Crossref’s detailed retraction status into a single Boolean `is_retracted` led to widespread misclassification of retracted vs. non‑retracted papers over several months, affecting any service using that field  (Hauschke & Nazarovets, 2024).  

### Duplicates, PIDs, and structural issues
- **Duplicate records sharing the same DOI** occur in all major databases to varying degrees (“identity cloning”), complicating matching and counts  (Culbert et al., 2024; Mongeon et al., 2025; Rizzetto & Peroni, 2023).  
- Cross‑collection mappings (OpenCitations Meta–OpenAlex) reveal **multi‑mapped identifiers**, sometimes due to different modeling of versions (preprint vs version of record) and sometimes due to upstream or ingest‑software errors  (Rizzetto & Peroni, 2023).  

### Effects of heterogeneous and uncurated sources
- Use of multiple heterogeneous sources and limited standardization at journal/publisher level leads to **loss of information and propagation of upstream errors** when building third‑party databases like OpenAlex  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Borrego & Urbano, 2025; Scheidsteger & Haunschild, 2023).  
- Evidence suggests a **trade‑off between coverage and metadata accuracy**: OpenAlex maximizes inclusiveness but has higher rates of missing affiliations, funding, references, and classification errors than Scopus/WoS, while still being strong in some fields like basic publication and author metadata  (Alperin et al., 2024; Scheidsteger & Haunschild, 2023; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  

## **Summary**

Scholarly aggregators ingest metadata mainly from publisher deposits (especially via Crossref) and other open/legacy sources, then algorithmically enrich, normalize, and link entities through PIDs and web crawling. Crossref itself passes through largely uncurated publisher data, so errors there propagate to OpenAlex, Scopus, and others. Reported problems include missing or inconsistent bibliographic fields, document type and language misclassification, incomplete abstracts, large gaps in institutional affiliations and funding, reference list and citation link omissions, DOI duplication, and mislabeling of retractions. OpenAlex, in particular, illustrates the challenges of combining heterogeneous open sources at scale: it achieves broad coverage and strong performance on some fields but exhibits higher volatility and error rates than more curated proprietary databases, requiring users to apply careful cleaning and cross‑validation.

## **Comparative Metadata Quality Studies between Sources and Aggregators**

There are several studies that explicitly compare metadata quality between “source” systems (publishers, repositories, or Crossref/DataCite as primary registries) and large aggregated or secondary scholarly databases.

## **Types of Comparative Studies**

### **Source vs. Multiple Aggregators**
- A large study draws a random sample of >115k Crossref records (as a neutral “source”) and checks how seven free databases (Dimensions, Google Scholar, Microsoft Academic, OpenAlex, Scilit, Semantic Scholar, The Lens) represent the same items, comparing **completeness and agreement** for seven metadata aspects (abstract, access, bibliographic info, document type, date, language, identifiers)  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024).  
- African publications are used to compare **coverage and metadata completeness/accuracy** of OpenAlex against Scopus, Web of Science, and AJOL (regional journal platform)  (Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- Reference coverage and selected core metadata (ORCID, abstracts, OA indicators) are compared across OpenAlex, Web of Science, and Scopus on a shared set of 16.8M articles  (Culbert et al., 2024).  
- Open metadata derived from Crossref/OpenAlex (hoaddata) is compared with Scopus and Web of Science for hybrid OA journals, assessing differences in coverage and metadata availability  (Jahn, 2025).  

### **Repositories and Registry Metadata**
- A multi-institutional analysis uses DataCite and Crossref APIs as aggregators to see where institutional datasets are deposited and to assess **missing fields (e.g., affiliations, funding)** important for public access; it notes discrepancies between source repositories and aggregators due to varying standards  (Johnston et al., 2024).  
- Quantitative assessments of research data repositories examine **completeness, description length, timeliness, and homogeneity** across repository types and certifications, using repository metadata as both object and comparator  (Strecker, 2021).  

## **Common Methodologies**

### **Record Matching and Sampling**
- Draw a **sample from a source system** (often Crossref or a specific repository set) and match records in aggregators via DOI or other identifiers  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Culbert et al., 2024).  
- Restrict to a **shared set of records** indexed in all databases being compared, then analyze field-level presence and values  (Culbert et al., 2024; Jahn, 2025; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  

### **Operational Quality Metrics**
- **Completeness rates**: proportion of records where a field is non-empty for each system  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Strecker, 2021; Culbert et al., 2024).  
- **Accuracy/agreement**: classify fields as fully matching, partially missing, or non-matching between source and aggregators  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Culbert et al., 2024).  
- **Coverage**: presence/absence of records (e.g., how many source items are found in each database) and of specific metadata types (affiliations, funders, references)  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024; Strecker, 2021; Jahn, 2025; Culbert et al., 2024).  
- **Timeliness**: lag between dataset publication and metadata registration, by repository type  (Strecker, 2021).

## **Comparing metadata quality across a national platform and a global index**

When both a national journal platform (e.g., DergiPark) and a global index have imperfect metadata, comparison relies on explicit quality dimensions, a neutral reference set, and systematic cross-checking rather than assuming either is correct.

## **1. Use a Third Source and Shared Identifiers**

- Draw a **DOI-based sample** from a neutral backbone such as Crossref (or from one system) and locate the same records in the other system  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Hendricks et al., 2020).  
- For each shared record, compare: presence of fields (completeness) and agreement of values (accuracy) across systems and against the article PDF or Crossref  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; C'espedes et al., 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- Large studies use exactly this approach to compare Crossref-based samples across multiple databases (OpenAlex, Scopus, WoS, etc.)  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; C'espedes et al., 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).

### Core cross-system metrics

| Aspect             | Example metric                                              | Citations |
|-------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----------|
| **Completeness**  | % of records with non‑empty field per system                |  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Kumar et al., 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024)|
| **Agreement**     | % of records where values match reference / full text       |  (C'espedes et al., 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024)|
| **Discrepancies** | Rates of complete missing, partial missing, non‑matching    |  (C'espedes et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024)|

**Figure 1:** Standard metrics for cross-database metadata comparison

## **2. Apply Standard Quality Dimensions**

Scoping work shows recurring dimensions: **completeness, accuracy, consistency, conformance, provenance, timeliness, accessibility**  (Kumar et al., 2024).  
Operationalizations used in large database comparisons include:

- **Completeness**: fill rates for abstracts, identifiers, dates, document type, language, affiliations, references  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; C'espedes et al., 2024; Culbert et al., 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- **Accuracy / error types**: classify each field as **full information, partially missing, completely missing, or non‑matching** against the article; this has been applied to OpenAlex vs Scopus/WoS  (C'espedes et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- **Consistency / conformance**: check internal coherence (e.g., single-issue journals and missing issue numbers, date policies) and adherence to expected schemas or vocabularies  (Delgado-Quirós & Ortega, 2024; Kubler et al., 2017; Culbert et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  

## **3. Interpreting Incomplete / Inconsistent Metadata**

- Treat neither system as a gold standard; instead, quantify where each performs better by field (e.g., publication and author data often strong in broad indexes, affiliations/funding weaker  (Culbert et al., 2024; Guerrero-Bote et al., 2021; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024)).  
- Analyze **patterns of missingness** (by year, journal, language, or publisher) to understand biases and limits on downstream analyses  (Culbert et al., 2024; Johnston et al., 2024; Alonso-Álvarez & Van Eck, 2024).  
- Use these metrics to decide when the national platform is the preferred source (e.g., richer local language or journal-level fields) and when the global index is preferable (e.g., PIDs, citation links).  

## **Summary**

Meaningful comparison between a national journal platform and a global index with imperfect metadata is possible by: (1) anchoring on a DOI-based sample and/or a neutral reference like Crossref, (2) measuring field-level completeness and agreement using standardized error categories, and (3) analyzing systematic discrepancy patterns instead of assuming either source is correct. This yields a nuanced picture of relative strengths and weaknesses across metadata fields and helps choose or combine sources appropriately.
